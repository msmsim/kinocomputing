Welcome to oncology

Oncology is the branch of medicine devoted to cancer, which is really hundreds of diseases sharing one flaw: cells that grow and divide without limit. Oncologists work out why this happens, how to catch it early and how to stop it.

Your body replaces billions of cells a day, and every division is tightly controlled. Cancer starts when that control breaks down, almost always because of damage to DNA.

Two kinds of gene matter most. Oncogenes work like an accelerator, and when they mutate it gets stuck to the floor. Tumour suppressor genes, like p53 and BRCA1, are the brakes. One faulty gene is rarely enough. A cancer usually needs several hits over many years, which is why risk climbs so steeply with age.

Some of that damage comes from outside: tobacco above all, then alcohol, obesity, sunlight, radiation and viruses like HPV. Some is inherited. And some is plain bad luck, the occasional typo that creeps in whenever DNA is copied.

Not every lump is cancer. A benign tumour stays put. A malignant one invades nearby tissue and can metastasise, sending cells through the blood or lymph to seed new tumours elsewhere. That spread, rather than the original tumour, causes most cancer deaths.

Cancers are named after where they start. Carcinomas grow from the cells lining organs and skin, and they include most breast, lung, bowel and prostate cancers. Sarcomas come from bone and muscle. Leukaemias and lymphomas arise in the blood and immune system.

Finding cancer early changes everything, which is the point of screening programmes such as mammograms and bowel tests. A biopsy confirms the diagnosis, and staging measures how far the cancer has gone. Stage I is small and contained, and stage IV has spread to distant organs. The stage drives nearly every decision that follows.

Treatment has three traditional pillars. Surgery cuts the tumour out, and for many early cancers that alone is a cure. Radiotherapy destroys cancer cells with focused beams. Chemotherapy poisons fast-dividing cells. That’s effective, but it hits healthy fast-dividing cells too, hence the hair loss and nausea.

The past two decades have added sharper tools. Targeted drugs block the specific faulty proteins that drive a particular tumour. Immunotherapy takes a different approach. Cancers often survive by hiding from the immune system, and these treatments strip away the disguise, sometimes producing lasting remissions in cancers once considered hopeless.

Most patients receive a mix of these, planned by a team of specialists. Palliative care, which focuses on comfort and quality of life, runs alongside treatment rather than only coming at the end.

The overall picture is better than many people assume. Survival rates have roughly doubled in the UK over the past fifty years. The gaps are still stark, though: testicular cancer is now usually curable, while pancreatic cancer remains brutally hard to treat.

The frontier is personalisation. Oncologists can now sequence a tumour’s DNA, detect traces of cancer in a blood sample, and even design vaccines against an individual patient’s mutations. Increasingly, the question is less “what cancer is this?” and more “what is driving this particular tumour?”
