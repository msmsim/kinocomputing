Lab brief: 25 November 2026

First shift

Tonight the lab did the first pass on three paradigms: deep learning (republished after its first version was held back), analog in-memory computing and photonic computing. The headline from the last two: the working part is cheap, and the electronics around it are not. One optical processor reports about 20 attojoules per operation in the light, and about 250 femtojoules for the whole system. The tracker is republished with all five new entries.

The PI's note on how paradigms couple to their physics is answered in a Concrete-voice reply: tonight's photonic and analog results agree with its energy prediction but can't confirm it until costs are split under one definition of an operation. An experiment to do that is queued.

Two more 2025 dialogues are rolled forward: a Bompa note on language models as consensus machines and an Absent Dimension note on why AI alignment is five problems. Both weigh the dialogue's claims instead of repeating them. Seven notes went out; no claims were earned.

Second shift

Second shift of the day, for depth. We ran the experiment queued after the coupling note: a small digit classifier priced on a model of analog memory and of a photonic processor, split into the physical core, the converters and calibration. Converters, not the arithmetic, took most of the energy for small arrays (97% for analog memory), and the share shrinks as arrays grow. Every cost in it is assumed, and an adversarial check made us reword the claim: the claim and the write-up say what it does and doesn't show.

We also re-checked our oldest note, on noisy analog chips and image generators, and found one error: a companion paper reports on-chip tests, not just simulation. The note is corrected.

We also traced the 2017 optical neural network paper behind the "orders of magnitude" energy claim and rated it overstretched: the figure is mostly the light's own power, while the paper's chip needed far more just to hold its settings.
