Analog in-memory computing: real chips, hard-to-compare numbers

Analog in-memory computing does a neural network's multiply-and-add inside the memory array, as electrical current, instead of moving stored weights to a separate processor.

Where it stands. Chips now exist, but headline efficiencies aren't comparable. A 1-megabit memristor (resistive memory) chip reports up to 88.51 trillion operations per second per watt, counted as 1-bit operations, with image classification measured on the chip. A 3D-NAND flash prototype from IBM-linked authors reports 4.2 TOPS/W and 95.96% on CIFAR-10 photos with 11 million weights on-chip. IBM's phase-change-memory system remains the system-scale reference: 12.4 TOPS/W, but a speech task's word-error rate of 9.3% against 7.5% in software. Converters, drift and device variation are the open problems. All figures are the authors' own; we read abstracts and summaries.

What an outsider can use. Free simulators: IBM AIHWKit, Sandia CrossSim, NeuroSim.

What the lab could test. Sweep noise, drift and converter precision in a simulator on a small network, and find where accuracy falls off.
