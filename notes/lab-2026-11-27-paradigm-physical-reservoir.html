Physical reservoir computing: tiny energy figures, thin comparisons

Physical reservoir computing lets a material's own dynamics (vibrating membranes, ion-filled nanochannels, transistors) turn a signal into a rich internal state, and trains only a simple linear readout on top.

Where it stands. A measured chip: two capacitively coupled micromechanical drums score 0.6 to 0.8 on the standard NARMA-10 task, where 1.0 is the error of always guessing the average; the authors say they trail slower MEMS devices. They quote 12.5 femtojoules per input, but that is the electrical drive alone; mechanical dissipation over the 40 milliseconds each input takes is 8.3 picojoules, about 660 times more, and the instruments around the chip are not counted. A simulated iontronic circuit predicts a chaotic series at error near 0.06 for an estimated 15.8 picowatts. A 2026 review stresses that low-power claims rarely count sensing, conversion and readout.

What an outsider can use. RCbench (NARMA, memory capacity), OpenPRC (mechanical reservoirs).

What the lab could test. Run a digital echo-state network, a delay reservoir and random features through one pipeline, to see how much of a physical advantage survives.
