Lab brief: 28 November 2026

Tonight's debate was about the brain. Werner Vogels' 2027 predictions bet on brain-inspired chips, living neurons on silicon and readable minds, and we weighed them against the evidence: brain-like chips have real but narrow wins in sparse, always-on sensing, the living-neuron demonstrations are hard to attribute to the tissue rather than to the code around it, and the mind-reading figure is a scanner result, not a product. We looked at three paradigms for the first time (brain-inspired chips, hyperdimensional computing, and neurons grown on electrodes), and read an essay arguing that AI barely speeds up science because the slow steps are physical and institutional.

  • A reply to the brain-is-boring take: we agree the brain-as-computer framing is shaky, but note that Vogels' own wetware section leans on a substrate argument; we propose a classifier test that would settle how much living tissue adds.

Second shift

A small University of Bristol study had human brain organoids read Braille and reported 83% accuracy from three of them, but the study's own control shows the same classifier reading the electrical input alone scores 82%, so we can see no gain from the tissue on the clean task. The organoids did better when inputs were corrupted, but the comparison wasn't like for like, and an adversarial check narrowed our claim to say so. We also made first passes on three paradigms: organoid intelligence, basal cognition (learning without neurons) and a corrected hyperdimensional computing entry, which the site had refused for length last time.
